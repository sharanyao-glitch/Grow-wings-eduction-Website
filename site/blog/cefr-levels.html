From A1 to C2: how to climb every CEFR level in English

Knowing your level is useful. Knowing what it takes to reach the next one is better. Here’s what changes at each step of the CEFR ladder, and how to get there.

The short answer

Moving up one CEFR level takes roughly 200 guided learning hours, and each jump asks for something different: words, then independence, then argument, then flexibility, then precision. Find your level for free with the EF SET, then practise exactly what your next jump needs.

If you’ve ever taken an English test, you’ve probably seen a result like “B1” or “B2” and wondered what it really means. More importantly, how do you get to the next one?

That label comes from the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages (CEFR), the international standard used by universities, employers and exam boards to describe language ability. The Council of Europe, which created the CEFR, places learners on six levels, from A1 to C2.

What are the six CEFR levels?

The six levels fall into three broad bands: Basic User, Independent User and Proficient User.

Most CEFR articles stop at describing the levels. This one is about the jumps: what actually separates one level from the next, and what to practise to move up.

How long does each jump take?

Cambridge English estimates that moving up one CEFR level takes roughly 200 guided learning hours: time in lessons plus supervised practice. Here are their approximate cumulative figures, counted from zero:

LevelTotal guided hours (approx.)What it feels like
A190–100Survival phrases, introductions
A2180–200Simple, routine conversations
B1350–400Handling everyday life and work on familiar topics
B2500–600Confident discussion, opinions, meetings
C1700–800Fluent, flexible, professional English
C21,000–1,200Near-effortless precision

Source: Cambridge English. These are guidelines; your time will vary with practice, exposure and how often you actually use English.

Notice that the gaps get bigger as you go up. Higher levels take longer because the improvements are subtler. You’re no longer learning new words for things; you’re learning to say things better.

Jump 1

A1 → A2: From words to sentences

The shift: At A1 you can introduce yourself and answer simple questions if the other person speaks slowly. At A2 you can manage short, routine exchanges on your own: shopping, directions, your job, your family.

What to focus on

  • Build core vocabulary by theme. Learn words in clusters (food, travel, office, family) rather than random lists. Aim for the most frequent 1,000 words.
  • Master the basic tenses. Present simple, present continuous, past simple and “going to” for plans. These four cover most everyday talk.
  • Learn phrases, not just words. “Could I have…”, “How much is…”, “I’m looking for…”. Whole chunks get you speaking faster than grammar rules do.
  • Listen to slow, clear English daily. Graded audio, learner podcasts or children’s shows with subtitles.
You’re ready for the next level when you can…
  • Describe your daily routine, your work and where you live in a few connected sentences
  • Order food, ask for directions or buy a ticket without switching languages
  • Write a short message or email to a colleague
Jump 2

A2 → B1: From survival to independence

The shift: This is the big one. A2 learners can handle predictable situations. B1 learners can handle the unpredictable: telling a story, explaining a problem, giving a reason for an opinion. B1 is where you become an “Independent User”.

What to focus on

  • Connect your ideas. Practise linking words: because, so, but, although, then, after that. B1 speech flows as connected ideas, not one sentence at a time.
  • Tell stories in the past. Add past continuous and present perfect (“I was working when…”, “I’ve worked here for three years”).
  • Speak every week, with feedback. At this stage, speaking practice matters more than more grammar study. Join a class, a speaking group or find a practice partner.
  • Read short real-world texts. News summaries, company emails, product reviews. Get used to English that wasn’t written for learners.
  • Stop translating in your head. Practise thinking in simple English: narrate what you’re doing as you do it.
You’re ready for the next level when you can…
  • Explain a problem at work and suggest a fix
  • Talk about your experiences, plans and opinions, and briefly say why
  • Follow the main points of a meeting on a familiar topic
Jump 3

B1 → B2: From getting by to holding your own

The shift: B1 is comfortable on familiar ground. B2 means you can discuss abstract and technical topics, argue a point, and talk with fluent speakers without either side straining. For most corporate roles, B2 is the level employers look for.

What to focus on

  • Learn to argue a point. Practise structuring opinions: state your view, give two reasons, address the other side, conclude. Debates and case discussions are ideal.
  • Grow your vocabulary in your field. Learn the language of your industry: the verbs, collocations and phrases your senior colleagues use.
  • Handle complex grammar naturally. Conditionals (“If we’d launched earlier…”), passive voice and reported speech should come out without stopping to think.
  • Consume native-speed content. Podcasts, TED talks, industry webinars, first with subtitles and then without.
  • Fix your fossilised errors. Many B1 learners repeat the same mistakes for years. Record yourself, or ask a trainer to point out your top five recurring errors, and work on them one at a time.
You’re ready for the next level when you can…
  • Present an idea in a meeting and handle questions
  • Write a clear, detailed email or report that explains pros and cons
  • Follow most of a film or podcast without subtitles

Jump 4

B2 → C1: From fluent to flexible

The shift: B2 speakers are fluent. C1 speakers are flexible: they shift tone for a client, a boss or a friend, pick up implied meaning, and rarely search for words. This is the jump from “good English” to “professional-grade English”.

What to focus on

  • Work on register and tone. Practise saying the same message three ways: formal, neutral and casual. Notice how senior leaders soften feedback or disagree politely.
  • Read between the lines. Long-form articles, opinion pieces and books. Ask yourself what the writer implies, not just what they say.
  • Use idioms and phrasal verbs accurately. Learn fewer, and use them well. One well-placed “let’s circle back” beats ten forced idioms.
  • Structure long-form speaking. Practise 5-minute talks with a clear opening, signposting (“There are three reasons…”) and a strong close.
  • Get expert feedback. Self-study gets harder to measure at this level. A trainer or coach can hear the subtle gaps you can’t.
You’re ready for the next level when you can…
  • Lead a meeting, negotiate or handle a difficult conversation in English
  • Understand jokes, sarcasm and implied criticism
  • Write well-structured, persuasive documents on complex subjects
Jump 5

C1 → C2: From advanced to masterful

The shift: C2 is about precision and nuance: choosing exactly the right word, summarising complex arguments from several sources, and expressing fine shades of meaning effortlessly. Few people need C2, but those who do (writers, academics, senior communicators) get a lot from it.

What to focus on

  • Read widely and critically. Literature, academic writing and quality journalism. Study how great writers build an argument.
  • Practise summarising and synthesising. Read three articles on one topic and present a single coherent view.
  • Refine style. Cut wordiness, vary sentence rhythm, and choose precise verbs over vague ones.
  • Live in the language. At this level, progress comes from immersion: working, debating and creating in English every day.

Which habits speed up every jump?

  1. 1

    Speak more than feels comfortable

    Passive study (reading, watching) builds understanding, but only speaking builds fluency.
  2. 2

    Practise little and often

    30 minutes a day beats a three-hour weekend cram. Language sticks through repetition.
  3. 3

    Measure, don’t guess

    Test yourself at the start and every few months, so you know whether your practice is working.
Trainer’s tipTry a daily one-minute recording. Each evening, record yourself talking for one minute about your day, without notes. Once a month, listen to the first and latest recordings side by side. It’s the easiest way to hear your own progress.
