Emotional intelligence at work: the real superpower

As more routine work gets automated, the human skills matter more than ever. Here is what emotional intelligence really means at work, and five everyday habits that help you build it.

The short answer

Emotional intelligence is being smart about feelings: noticing your own, managing them, and reading and responding well to others’. It shapes how people handle pressure, feedback and teamwork, and unlike many technical skills, it doesn’t go out of date.

Think of the best colleague you have ever worked with. Chances are you don’t remember them for their spreadsheets. You remember how they stayed calm in a crisis, how they made you feel heard, or how they gave tough feedback kindly.

That is emotional intelligence at work. And it is a skill, which means it can be learned.

What is emotional intelligence, really?

Emotional intelligence, often called EQ, is the ability to recognise and manage emotions, in yourself and in others. It isn’t about being soft or always positive. It is about responding thoughtfully instead of reacting automatically.

Why does emotional intelligence matter more now?

As technology takes on more routine work, the human parts of the job grow in importance: building trust, working through disagreement, supporting a stressed colleague, leading through change. These are exactly the moments where emotional intelligence shows.

What are the four parts of emotional intelligence?

Most models describe four connected skills:

  • Self-awareness. Knowing what you are feeling and how it affects your work and decisions.
  • Self-management. Staying steady under pressure and choosing how you respond.
  • Social awareness. Reading the room, and noticing what others feel even when they don’t say it.
  • Relationship skills. Using all of the above to communicate, resolve conflict and work well together.

How do you build emotional intelligence?

Like any skill, it grows with small, regular practice. These five habits are a good place to start:

  1. 1

    Name the feeling

    Go beyond “fine” or “stressed”. Is it worry, disappointment, impatience? Naming a feeling makes it easier to manage.
  2. 2

    Pause before you reply

    When an email or comment stings, wait a few minutes before responding. The pause is where better choices live.
  3. 3

    Get curious about others

    When someone reacts strongly, ask yourself what might be going on for them before you decide what it means.
  4. 4

    Listen to understand

    In your next conversation, try to summarise what the other person said before giving your view.
  5. 5

    Ask for feedback

    Ask a trusted colleague, “How do I come across when I’m under pressure?” Then simply say thank you.
Trainer’s tipTry a daily mood check-in. At the end of each workday, write one word for how you felt and one moment that caused it. In our workshops we use a mood-o-meter wall for this. After a couple of weeks, most people start spotting patterns they never noticed before.

How long does it take to build emotional intelligence?

Small changes, like pausing before replying, can show up within days. Deeper habits, such as staying calm in conflict or giving difficult feedback well, build over months of practice. The key is regular reflection: noticing what happened, and what you would try next time.

The bottom line

Emotional intelligence is not a soft extra; it is what makes teams work. It can be learned, one small habit at a time. Start today by naming one feeling, and pausing once before you reply.
